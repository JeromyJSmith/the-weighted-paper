How it works

You are scored on what you would do with this client, now

What the sitting is

The Certificate of Proficiency puts you in counselling situations and scores the action you take. BACP says it tests generic principles that apply across ways of working (BACP).

Each item is a moment in the work: first contact, contracting, a disclosure, a request from a manager, an ending. You choose what you would do. The score moves when you know the order of things, and when you keep a promise you can actually keep.

How a case is built

Six case studies of practice, written by practising counsellors and psychotherapists, online, with up to two hours unless a reasonable adjustment applies (BACP online assessments). One candidate account puts the total at 43 questions (Counselling Tutor). Each case is a client over time. Each section is one moment in that work.

Once you confirm a section you cannot change it. Later sections are asked of the practitioner who already made those choices.

A coherent line of practice scores better than six clever but unrelated answers.

Aim at two-thirds of a section

BACP publishes no overall mark. Results come within five weeks after monthly moderation. What you can rely on is the ratio in their own worked example: a section with a maximum of 6 and a pass mark of 4. Two-thirds. Aim there. One difficult item does not define the sitting.

Six questions for every option

Run each option through these before you confirm. They fit on a scrap of paper.

  1. Now? Does it address these facts, at this stage?
  2. Collaborative? Is the client in the decision where they can be?
  3. Proportionate? Does it match the level of risk and evidence?
  4. Transparent? Are limits and next steps named clearly?
  5. In role? Does it respect the contract, setting and your competence?
  6. Accountable? Could you record it and take it to supervision?

Four weights

BACP’s published example shows that options carry different weights. In the sample Multiple Decision-Making section, two statements score 2 points each and are described as the most appropriate responses; two more sit above the pass mark as bonus points; and negative-scoring options subtract (BACP how-to guide).

Serious−2Harm or a broken dutyA promise you cannot keep, or a disclosure you should not make

Warmth does not set the weight. Consequence does. A response can be client-centred and still miss what this client needed in this moment.

Ticking everything scores zero

In BACP’s sample section, the positive scores total +6 and the negative scores total −6, so selecting every statement produces exactly zero.

Try it. This sample has nine boxes (V4). Tick everything that looks like good practice and watch the score go to zero.

    Nothing selected yet.

    In the room you would never do all nine of those things at once. The sitting asks what you would do. Approving of an option is not the same as choosing it.

    Read the instruction before the options. Multiple Decision-Making invites as many responses as you consider essential. Single Decision-Making asks for one. In BACP’s example the correct single answer scores +2 and each wrong answer costs −2.

    Bonus points

    BACP describes the two points above the pass mark as bonus points that can help counteract negative answers selected elsewhere in the assessment. They offset the arithmetic. They do not repair a promise of absolute confidentiality, or any other judgement you should not have made.

    Information Gathering

    These sections ask what you would explore. Positive picks tell you more about the client. BACP states that passing does not depend on obtaining that extra information, because the necessary information to complete each section is always supplied.

    Ask about the client’s safety, hopes and context. Leave the ex-partner’s motives, the other pupil’s version of the fight, and the colleague who caused the restructure. Those people are not your clients, and you have privacy duties toward them.

    In the work

    What the cases keep asking

    These judgements come up across a sitting.

    Name confidentiality limits at contracting

    Explain the limits when you agree the work, while the room is still ordinary. Naming them for the first time in the middle of a disclosure damages trust. “Everything you say stays in this room” is a promise you cannot keep.

    Raise a safeguarding concern. Do not investigate.

    Your threshold is concern. Establishing what really happened (dates, corroboration, a fuller account) belongs to the designated safeguarding lead and statutory services.

    Stay with the client on risk

    Acting over them (phoning the GP without telling them, escalating on one ambiguous sentence) takes them out of the decision. Changing the subject, or promising never to mention it again, does the same. Keep them in it.

    Competence is training, supervision, or someone else

    Wanting to help does not extend your scope. The client’s need does not either. What extends it is training, supervision, and referral when the work is beyond you.

    Keep personal and professional channels separate

    Personal social media, informal check-ins, dual relationships: the first exception makes the second easier to ask for. Hold the working agreement.

    Records are for the work, and for accountability

    Factual, proportionate, secure. Written so the work can continue and so you can account for it. A full transcript and an empty file both fail that.

    A signature is not the working agreement

    Consent is what you have both understood and agreed. Give her a record of it. Go over fees, endings and confidentiality limits in the room. Holding the work hostage for a returned form confuses admin with consent.

    The commissioner is not the client

    In school, EAP and agency work, the payer may know about attendance, fees and whether the person showed up. Session content stays with the client unless the working agreement says otherwise.

    Endings need notice and a way onward

    Unplanned endings, endings by email, endings the client chooses: give reasonable notice where you can, say how records and complaints work, and offer accurate onward options. Do not talk them into staying.

    Section types

    How a section is built

    There are eight mixes: V1 through V8. That is the whole list. Every mix uses the same four weights — Core +2, Bonus +1, Poor −1, Serious −2 — in a different combination. The mix tells you what the section is testing.

    V4 is called nine-option because that mix puts nine boxes on the page: 2 Core + 2 Bonus + 4 Poor + 1 Serious = 9. The goods add to +6. The bads add to −6. Tick all nine and you score 0. It is not a missing V9.

    Reading the shape from the option list

    You counted nine boxesV4 — nine-option, because 2+2+4+1=9Goods +6, bads −6. Tick all nine and you score 0. Pick two, maybe four.
    Five options, two of them alarming in opposite directionsV2 loss-weightedBoth positives are compulsory. One wrong pick fails it.
    “Select one option only”V3 or V6Find the option that acts and checks. If none does, find the one that does least harm.
    Three options that all sound obligatoryV7 core-heavyThey probably all are. Take them.
    Lots of mild good practice, one jarring optionV8 bonus-richFind the one mandatory act first, then bank.
    “Which areas would you want to explore”V5 Information GatheringAsk about the client. Leave third parties.

    In-test tactics

    Pace at twenty minutes per case

    Six cases, two hours. You may split the time however you like, but the twenty-minute rhythm is the sanity check that stops case one eating case six.

    Read the selection instruction before the options

    Half of all avoidable losses are people multi-selecting a single-decision section, or picking one thing in a multi-decision one.

    Count the boxes first

    Nine boxes is V4, the zero-sum mix: be sparing. Five with two alarming opposites is V2: both positives are compulsory. Three seconds, and it reframes the whole section.

    Run every option through six questions

    Is it indicated now, given these facts? Is it proportionate? Does it keep the client in the decision? Does it clarify rather than assume? Is it within my competence? Would it survive being described in supervision?

    Distrust absolutes

    “Always,” “never,” “guarantee,” “immediately terminate,” “nothing will ever leave this room.” An option containing one of these is usually the −2.

    Distrust generosity

    The kindest-sounding option in a single-decision section is very often the trap: free sessions, staying in touch informally, making an exception because the reason is sympathetic. Warmth is not a boundary.

    Separate the immediate act from the eventual one

    Many wrong options are right things done at the wrong time: investigating before reporting, referring before discussing, contacting a third party before speaking to the client.

    Commit deliberately

    Confirmed selections cannot be changed, though you can review earlier parts of the current case for context. Pause for one breath before each confirm.

    Do not expect a score

    Results are Pass or Fail, moderated monthly, communicated within five weeks. Nothing is shown on the day.
